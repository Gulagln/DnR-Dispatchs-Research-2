Every few months, a new version of the same chart makes the rounds: the top handful of names now account for a share of index weight not seen in decades, and the caption underneath is always some variant of "this ends badly." It's a reasonable instinct. It's also, I think, the wrong way to evaluate the setup.

## The chart everyone shows, and what it leaves out

Weight concentration by itself tells you almost nothing about whether a trade is late. What it doesn't show is the denominator that actually matters: what share of aggregate earnings growth those same names are producing. A market where the top ten names are 35% of the index but generating 55% of earnings growth is a very different animal from one where that same weight is producing 20% of growth. The first is concentration catching up to fundamentals with a lag. The second is a bubble.

Right now, on the numbers I track, we're closer to the first case than the second — not because valuation is cheap (it isn't, in absolute terms), but because the growth contribution has kept pace with the weight gain in a way that wasn't true in prior concentration episodes people like to cite as cautionary tales.

## Where the historical comparison breaks down

The 2000 comparison gets used constantly, and it's the weakest of the available analogies. That episode was concentration built on revenue multiples with no earnings underneath them — story stocks, in the literal sense. What's different this time isn't that "it's different," the most overused four words in markets — it's that the current leadership group is, on average, converting revenue growth into free cash flow at a rate the 2000 cohort never approached, even at the peak of that cycle's enthusiasm.

The better comparison, if you want a historical anchor, is closer to the industrial and oil concentration of the 1970s: a small number of companies capturing an outsized share of a genuine structural shift, with the market slow to reprice how durable that share actually was until well after the trend was obvious. Those episodes didn't end because concentration itself was the problem. They ended when the underlying growth driver matured and the market was still pricing it as if it hadn't.

## What would actually change my mind

I'm not making the case that this never ends — every concentration cycle eventually does. I'm making the narrower case that weight alone is a poor timing signal, and that the more useful thing to watch is the gap between earnings-growth contribution and index-weight contribution. Right now that gap is narrow. It's worth revisiting this view any time that gap starts widening — growth contribution rolling over while weight keeps climbing is the actual warning sign, not the weight number by itself.

None of this is a call to buy or sell anything specific. It's an argument for watching a different number than the one that gets circulated the most.
